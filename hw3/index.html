In this assignment, I implemented a physically-based renderer using a Monte Carlo pathtracing algorithm. I started with camera ray generation and analytic intersection algorithms for triangles and spheres, using this to create a Bounding Volume Hierarchy to lower rendering times from linear to logarithmic complexity.

Ater intersections, I introduced realistic shading with direct lighting computations and compared uniform hemisphere sampling against the more efficient importance sampling. Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation and Primitive Intersection Pipeline

The rendering pipeline begins by generating viewing rays for each pixel. Given unnormalized image coordinates $(x, y)$, the coordinates are first normalized and mapped to a virtual sensor plane located at $Z = -1$ in the camera's local coordinate system. The camera sensor coordinates $(X_c, Y_c)$ are determined by the field-of-view angles:

$$X_c = (2x - 1) \tan\left(\frac{\text{hFov}}{2}\right)$$ $$Y_c = (2y - 1) \tan\left(\frac{\text{vFov}}{2}\right)$$

In order to implement anti-aliasing, multiple sub-pixel samples are drawn randomly. This local directional vector is transformed into world space using the camera-to-world rotation matrix (c2w). A Ray object is constructed originating at the camera's world position and cast into the scene.

As the ray traverses the scene, it tests for collisions against primitives. The ray's intersection interval is defined by min_t and max_t. Each valid intersection updates max_t to the new hit time, so subsequent tests only register closer objects. On a confirmed hit, the surface normal and BSDF material properties are used for shading calculations.

Triangle Intersection Algorithm

I handled triangle intersection via the Möller-Trumbore algorithm which computes the intersection time $t$ and barycentric coordinates $(b_1, b_2)$ simultaneously by solving a linear system using Cramer's rule. This bypasses the need to explicitly compute the plane equation.

$$ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{S_1} \cdot \mathbf{E_1}} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{S_2} \cdot \mathbf{E_2} \\ \mathbf{S_1} \cdot \mathbf{S} \\ \mathbf{S_2} \cdot \mathbf{D} \end{bmatrix} $$

Where $\mathbf{E_1}$ and $\mathbf{E_2}$ are edge vectors, $\mathbf{S}$ is the translation vector from the vertex to the ray origin, and $\mathbf{D}$ is the ray direction. If the scalar divisor $\mathbf{S_1} \cdot \mathbf{E_1}$ is zero, the ray is parallel to the triangle. The intersection is rejected if $t$ falls outside the ray's valid min_t and max_t bounds, or if the barycentric coordinates fall outside the range $[0, 1]$ (or their sum exceeds 1). If valid, the exact normal is derived by interpolating the three vertex normals.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction and My Splitting Heuristic

I constructed the BVH using a recursive spatial partitioning algorithm. For a given set of primitives, a single bounding box encompassing all primitives is computed as well as a secondary bounding box strictly containing the centroids of those primitives. If the primitive count is less than or equal to max_leaf_size, recursion terminates and a leaf node is created.

In internal nodes, the splitting heuristic relies on the longest axis of the centroid bounding box. The algorithm calculates the midpoint along this axis and uses std::partition to divide the primitives into "left" and "right" subsets based on whether a primitive's centroid falls below or above this midpoint. Through this heuristic the tree is balanced spatially. An edge case handler splits the array precisely in half if all centroids lie exactly on the split point to prevent infinite recursion.

Performance Analysis

Without BVH acceleration, ray intersection operates at $O(n)$ complexity and scales linearly with the scene's primitive count. Rendering the cow.dae scene required approximately 40 seconds. By implementing the BVH, the complexity drops to $O(\log n)$ because the algorithm quickly prunes massive sections of unhit geometry via Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) intersection tests. With the BVH enabled, cow.dae rendered in roughly 0.17 seconds. Scenes like CBlucy.dae of hundreds of thousands of triangles went from taking hours to completing in under 2 seconds.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Implementations of Direct Lighting

The general reflection equation we approximate is:

$$ L_{out}(p, \omega_{out}) = \int_{H^2} L_{in}(p, \omega_{in}) f_r(p, \omega_{in}, \omega_{out}) \cos\theta_i \, d\omega_{in} $$

Uniform Hemisphere Sampling: This approach estimates direct lighting by generating random rays distributed uniformly across the hemisphere positioned directly over the intersection point. If a sampled ray hits an emissive object, its radiance is accumulated using the Monte Carlo estimator:

$$ L_{out} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{L_{in} f_r \cos\theta_i}{1 / 2\pi} $$

Because many sample rays hit empty space or non-emissive walls, this method requires a massive number of samples to converge and naturally produces a large amount of noise.

Importance Sampling (Light Sampling): This method directly samples the light sources in the scene. For each light, it draws a sample direction pointing from the hit point directly to the light. A shadow ray is then cast. If the ray reaches the light without hitting obstructing geometry, the unoccluded light's radiance is accumulated via the reflection equation and weighted by the specific probability density function $p(\omega_i)$ provided by the light.

$$ L_{out} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{L_{in} f_r \cos\theta_i}{p(\omega_i)} $$

Direct Lighting Comparison Images

Noise Levels in Soft Shadows (Importance Sampling)

When sampling an area light with a low ray count (e.g., 1 light ray), the penumbra of the shadow shows severe noise (binary black and white dots). This is because a single sample either entirely hits the area light or is entirely occluded by the sphere. As the number of light rays increases, the Monte Carlo estimator accurately captures the fractional visibility of the area light, generating a continuous and soft shadow gradient.

Uniform Hemisphere vs. Light Sampling

Uniform hemisphere sampling is very inefficient because it blindly fires rays. Since light sources usually make up a small fraction of the solid angle above a given surface, the vast majority of uniform rays don't contribute to the direct lighting integral and create heavy noise. Importance sampling restrains the sampling domain to known light sources. Every generated ray guarantees a potential light contribution (barring occlusion), so drastically smoother images are generated in the same amount time.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Indirect Lighting Implementation

The indirect lighting function recursively evaluates the rendering equation. For each hit point, direct illumination is calculated. If the path is permitted to continue (decided by max_ray_depth and an unbiased Russian Roulette coin flip of $c = 0.7$) a new incoming direction is sampled from the surface's BSDF.

$$ L_{indirect} = \frac{L_{incoming} \cdot f_r \cdot \cos\theta_i}{pdf \cdot c} $$

A new ray is cast and call the function recursively in order to receive the incoming indirect radiance from the next intersection. Dividing by the continuation probability $c$ unbiases the estimator.

Direct vs. Indirect Illumination

The direct illumination render has harsh and pitch-black shadows with flat lighting because it only captures light traveling straight from the area light onto the surface. The indirect illumination render is dim but softly lit. It captures color bleeding (the red and blue light bouncing off the walls onto the balls) and fills in the spaces where direct light cannot reach.

m-th Bounce of Light (Accumulated)

The unaccumulated images show isolated light paths of a specific length and the accumulated images show the sum of all light paths up to length m. m=0 is only the light. m=1 is direct lighting. m=2 has soft ambient lighting. Higher values of m (3, 4, 5) add diminishing returns of brightness to the scene.

Russian Roulette for Varying Depths

Russian Roulette allows rays to trace infinitely in theory, but probabilistically terminates them to save compute. For low m limits, the images look identical to the standard accumulated images because the maximum depth acts is a hard cap. However, at m=100, Russian Roulette prevents an infinite loop. The m=100 image appears identical to m=4 or m=5 as light energy dissipates after a few bounces and termination by the cpdf doesn't visually change the image much.

Sample Rate Comparison

With 1 sample per pixel, the image is overwhelmed by Monte Carlo noise because a single path cannot integrate the hemisphere of incoming light. For higher rates the noise is significantly reduced, and at 1024 samples the Monte Carlo integration converges to give a smooth and photorealistic image.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

My Adaptive Sampling Implementation

Adaptive sampling dynamically allocates compute by terminating the sampling loop early for converged pixels. Regions that face light sources converge quickly, while shadows and caustics need more samples to remove variance.

The algorithm keeps running sums of the pixel's illuminance ($s_1$) and squared illuminance ($s_2$). Convergence is tested iteratively in batches. During a test, the mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ are computed:

$$ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n} $$ $$ \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \left( s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n} \right) $$

A 95% confidence interval is evaluated with the metric $I$:

$$ I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} $$

If $I \le \text{maxTolerance} \cdot \mu$ then the pixel has converged. The sampling loop breaks and the average radiance is stored. The sampleCountBuffer is updated with the precise number of rays executed to output the rate map.

Adaptive Sampling Results

For the rate maps, blue areas have low sampling rates and early convergence while red areas need higher samples. Computational effort is concentrated under shadow penumbras and near colored walls where indirect lighting variance is highest.
