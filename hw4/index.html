Overview

In this project, I created a real-time physically-based cloth simulation built on a discrete mass-spring system. The architecture models the cloth as a 2D grid of interconnected PointMass objects governed by Newtonian mechanics. The core engine applies explicit numerical integration (Verlet integration) to advance the state of the system over discrete time steps while preserving positional constraints in order to prevent unnatural elasticity.

In addition to spring dynamics, I implemented robust spatial hashing to handle $O(n)$ self-collision resolution and primitive-based collision detection for interacting with static environments. To overcome the bottleneck of rendering with a CPU, I wrote GLSL shader programs to parallelize the rendering pipeline onto the GPU. This allows real-time evaluation of the Blinn-Phong reflection model, UV texture mapping, TBN matrix transformations for bump and displacement mapping, and environment-mapped cube sampling.

Part 1: Masses and Springs

The cloth is a discrete $N_w \times N_h$ grid of point masses. The 1D underlying memory array is in row-major order, so any discrete grid coordinate $(x, y)$ is mapped to a linear index via the standard transformation:

$$index = y \times N_w + x$$

After instantiation, cloth properties are defined by springs representing structural, shearing, and bending constraints. Structural springs enforce horizontal and vertical integrity by connecting masses at $(x, y)$ to $(x \pm 1, y)$ and $(x, y \pm 1)$. Shearing springs resist diagonal deformation by connecting $(x, y)$ to $(x \pm 1, y \pm 1)$. Bending constraints prevent sharp folding by connecting masses across a step size of two: $(x \pm 2, y)$ and $(x, y \pm 2)$.

Part 2: Simulation via Numerical Integration

The mass-spring system is advanced by computing the total force acting on each point mass and applying numerical integration. The total force $F_{total}$ is the sum of uniform external forces (gravity) $F_{ext} = m \cdot a_{ext}$ and the internal spring correction forces calculated using Hooke's Law:

$$F_s = k_s \left(||p_a - p_b|| - l\right) \frac{p_a - p_b}{||p_a - p_b||}$$

where $k_s$ is the stiffness coefficient and $l$ is the resting length of the spring. Once total forces are accumulated, I utilize explicit Verlet integration to compute the new positions. Verlet integration approximates velocity based on the position differential between the current and previous timestep and grants better numerical stability than forward Euler methods. I also incorporate an explicit damping term $d$ to simulate energy dissipation (e.g., air friction and heat loss):

$$x_{t+dt} = x_t + (1 - d) (x_t - x_{t-dt}) + a_t dt^2$$

To prevent chaotic behavior arising from numerical instability when springs stretch excessively in a single timestep, I applied a SIGGRAPH 1995 Provot deformation constraint, capping maximum spring deformation at $1.1 \times l$.

Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

Sphere Collisions

Collision detection against the spherical primitive evaluates the implicit equation of the sphere. If the Euclidean distance $||p - c|| \le r$, a collision occurs. The point is projected to the tangent surface by extending the normalized direction vector:

$$p_{tangent} = c + r \frac{p - c}{||p - c||}$$

The corrective displacement vector is scaled by the friction coefficient $(1 - f)$ to prevent infinite sliding.

Plane Collisions

Plane collision assumes an infinite boundary surface and evaluates if a particle traversed the boundary during timestep $dt$. I compute the dot product of the surface normal $n$ against both the previous and current position vectors relative to a point on the plane. A sign change indicates intersection. The point is then pushed out along the normal vector, offset by a minor epsilon to prevent floating-point Z-fighting.

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

Naive self-collision checks require pairwise comparisons for all points, yielding $O(n^2)$ computational complexity which is intractable for real-time simulation. To resolve this, I use a spatial hashing structure mapping $3D$ coordinate space to discrete volumetric bins.

The dimensions of the bounding boxes $(w, h, t)$ are statically derived from the mesh density. Points are assigned a unique hash $H(x, y, z)$. During the simulation step, point masses only evaluate repulsive distance constraints $||p_a - p_b|| < 2 \times \text{thickness}$ against candidates within the exact same spatial hash bucket, accelerating the intersection evaluation to $O(n)$. The resulting repulsive vectors are averaged and applied directly to the position array.

Part 5: Shaders

To overcome the CPU rendering bottleneck, I created customized GLSL programs (Vertex and Fragment shaders) executed via OpenGL. The Vertex Shader runs per-vertex and computes necessary coordinate transformations (e.g., model-to-screen space via projection matrices) and passing linearly interpolated data (normals, UV coordinates, tangents) to the next pipeline stage. The Fragment Shader processes fragments post-rasterization and applies mathematical lighting models and sampling texture arrays to make the final pixel output.

Blinn-Phong Shading Model

The Blinn-Phong shading model is a reflection model that approximates the behavior of light without the massive computational overhead of explicit ray-tracing. It computes final illumination $L$ as a linear combination of ambient, diffuse, and specular components:

$$L = k_a I_a + k_d \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, n \cdot l) + k_s \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, n \cdot h)^p$$

Where $n$ is the surface normal, $l$ is the illumination vector, and $h$ is the half-vector bisecting the viewer and light source, calculated as $h = \frac{v + l}{||v + l||}$.

Bump & Displacement Mapping

These techniques utilize a scalar height map $h(u,v)$ to perturb geometry, mapped via the Tangent-Bitangent-Normal ($\mathbf{TBN}$) transformation matrix which converts local tangent space vectors into model space: $\mathbf{TBN} = [t \quad b \quad n]$.

Bump mapping computes a local space normal $n_o = (-dU, -dV, 1)$ based on height differentials, transforming it via $n_d = \mathbf{TBN} n_o$. This creates a purely optical illusion of surface depth by altering fragment normals and the actual vertex geometry is unchanged.

Displacement mapping computes the same lighting adjustments but perturbs the physical geometry in the vertex shader: $p' = p + n \cdot h(u,v) \cdot k_h$, only changing the object's silhouette boundaries.

Environment-Mapped Reflections

The Mirror shader bypasses explicit path-tracing by sampling a pre-computed environment cubemap. It casts an eye-ray $w_o$ to the fragment position and computes its reflection vector $w_i$ across the surface normal. I query the cubemap texture along $w_i$ to simulate a perfect specular surface.
